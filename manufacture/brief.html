01

Year One

The year is the product.

We’re exploring Halo as a year-long membership for people making a serious commitment to themselves. The core is a structured Year One and an intentional network: people to meet, learn from and turn to for support. A private digital platform helps hold it together. The bracelet is the lasting artifact of the year they live.

What feels increasingly clear

The bracelet should earn a place among desirable luxury jewelry and withstand a year or more of frequent wear. Twelve genuine stones mark earned progress. Human connection and recognition belong to the membership, though the network and Year One program still need to be designed and tested. An AI companion is optional, not the center of it.

What we figure out together

Metal, construction, stones, settings, sequence, clasp, fit, durability, how progress changes the bracelet, suppliers and target cost. Brushed steel, silver and other approaches are all worth comparing. Your eye can help us find what someone would actually love to wear.

You can buy admission to Halo. You cannot buy completion. A finished bracelet should reflect a year lived, not simply a higher price. Another member might one day see it and understand: we’re doing this too.

02

Earned progress

Choose the twelve stones together.

The stones should follow Halo’s own Year One journey. Progress should change the object; a member should not simply receive twelve decorations for paying. The exact stones, order, meanings and physical earning or setting mechanics are open. Year One should be a broad personal-development framework, not an AA derivative, sobriety program or clinical treatment. Its curriculum still needs thoughtful work.

Current candidates to review: Moonstone, Amethyst, Turquoise, Rose Quartz, Carnelian, Lapis Lazuli, Green Aventurine, Tiger’s Eye, Black Tourmaline, Citrine, Clear Quartz and Opal. This is a starting set, not twelve locked choices.

Emerald might belong in month four, another month, or not at all. Its place should come from Halo’s story and the material itself, not another tradition’s sequence.

What I’d love your view on
  • Which stones feel right in person, and which would you replace?
  • What cuts, treatments, natural variation and setting needs should we expect?
  • How could twelve physical chapters feel coherent without deciding the story too early?
03

Jewelry first

Make it worth wearing every day.

Jewelry is Halo’s first physical expression, not the limit of the company. The bracelet is a membership object within a culture, not merchandise for an app. It should be beautiful before anyone knows what it means, yet recognizable to another member without obvious branding. Publicly desirable. Privately meaningful.

Stones

Help us find reliable sources for genuine natural stones. Let’s compare how each candidate looks in person, what treatments it may have, its availability, approximate cost and a good alternative.

Bracelet

Help compare brushed stainless steel, silver and other metals, makers and processes. Weight, finish, closure, secure settings, comfort and a year or more of wear matter more than a premature material label. How might another member recognize it? How should a completed Halo feel beside a new one?

Let’s understand the object we want to make, prototype it, then set a target cost with real supplier evidence. We are not designing backward from the cheapest possible bracelet.

04

What comes next

Start with samples and honest questions.

Let’s explore materials, stones, settings and makers together. The Year One curriculum and human network need their own careful design; we do not need to solve them in this jewelry brief. A small founding cohort, and perhaps later gatherings, could help us learn together. Neither is a decided launch plan. The copy and digital scenes can follow the object and the year we choose.

We should learn whether people want to wear the bracelet every day and whether the year and its people make it more meaningful. The year, the people, the commitment, the progress, then the object: each should make the next more meaningful.

Pricing and category questions we’re testing

We are exploring a luxury-priced Year One membership, not setting a final price. Approximately $500–1,000+ is a premium hypothesis, subject to market validation. Earlier accessible (~$195–295), premium (~$595) and luxury (~$995) studies can still test distinct propositions, but they are not launch tiers or one bracelet shown at three prices. First ask what kind of product people believe Halo is; then learn what value that category can support.
